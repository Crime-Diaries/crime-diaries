STATION 01 · CRYPTANALYSIS

Cipher room

What investigators do. When a message is written in code, analysts hunt for patterns. In English a few letters (E, T, A, O) turn up far more often than the rest, and a simple shift cipher keeps that pattern: it only moves it along the alphabet. Line the message's letter counts up with normal English and the key often falls out.

Intercepted message

Your decoding · shift 0

Limits of the method

Frequency analysis needs enough text: in a short message the counts are too few to trust. Ciphers that use several symbols for the same letter (homophonic ciphers) flatten the pattern on purpose. The Zodiac's 340-character cipher worked that way and was solved only in 2020, by volunteers using computer search.

STATION 02 · FINGERPRINTS

Print comparison

What investigators do. Examiners compare a latent print (a partial, often smudged mark left on a surface) with known prints on file. They follow the overall ridge flow first, then compare minutiae: the points where a ridge ends or forks. Position, type and direction all have to agree.

○ ridge ending · □ fork

Limits of the method

Real latent prints are partial and distorted, so the examiner judges how much agreement is enough; many countries no longer use a fixed number of points. That judgement can fail. In 2004 the FBI wrongly matched a print from the Madrid train bombings to Oregon lawyer Brandon Mayfield. Spanish police matched it to someone else, and the FBI later apologised.

A common safeguard is verification by a second examiner who does not know the first result.

STATION 03 · DNA PROFILING

DNA lab

What investigators do. DNA profiling compares short tandem repeats (STRs): places in the genome where a short sequence repeats a different number of times in different people. Everyone carries two versions (alleles) at each place, one from each parent. The lab reads the repeat counts as peaks on an electropherogram and compares the profile from a scene sample with reference samples. Real casework uses 20 or more places (loci); this lab uses 4.

Limits of the method

A clean sample from one person is the easy case. Mixtures of several people and very small (low-template) samples are hard to interpret, and analysts can disagree about them. A match says whose DNA it may be, not how or when it got there: DNA can move through touch and shared objects. Close relatives also share many alleles.
